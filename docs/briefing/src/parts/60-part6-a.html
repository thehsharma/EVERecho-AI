Part VI

Your operating system

The cadence that keeps this on track, the first thirty days in detail, and every decision currently waiting on you.

20  The cadence

Founders do not fail from lack of effort; they fail from losing the thread. A fixed cadence is how you notice a problem in week three instead of month five.

Every Monday — 45 minutes, alone

  1. Read the audit trail. Not a summary — the actual log, including refusals. Your own readiness document names this as a condition for piloting, and it is also how you find risk 7 before someone else does. Fifteen minutes.
  2. The five metrics from §12. Written down, in the same place, every week, so the trend is visible rather than remembered.
  3. The abstention band. Movement in either direction is an incident until proven otherwise.
  4. Open decisions (§22). Anything sitting for more than two weeks is either not important or being avoided. Decide which, out loud.
  5. One sentence: what is the single most important thing this week? Write it where you will see it on Thursday.

Every month — 2 hours

  • Cohort margin. The §2 margin trap alarm. If it is not improving, say so plainly in writing rather than assuming next month.
  • Unit costs against reality. Provider invoices attributed to archives. Hours logged and costed, including yours.
  • The falsification thresholds from §8. Reread them. They were written by a more objective version of you.
  • One customer conversation you did not need to have. Not a support ticket — a conversation.
  • Update the decision log. The company deserves the same discipline the codebase got.

Every quarter — half a day

  • Are you at the stage you thought? Against §16, honestly.
  • Risk register review (§18). Likelihoods change. So do the early-warning signs.
  • The refusal audit. Has anything shipped that weakens it? Do the enforcing tests still exist and still run? Has anyone asked you to remove it, and what did you say?
  • Governance progress (§17). Which instruments are in place, which are still intentions.
  • Your own state. Risk 5 is you. It is a real line item and it does not appear on any dashboard.
Keep a decision log for the company

The repository has one — fifteen decisions, each with its reasoning, which is why the architecture is still coherent. Do the same for the business: the decision, the date, the reasoning, what would change your mind. In eighteen months you will not remember why you chose a price, a channel or a jurisdiction, and the reasoning is worth more than the choice.

21  The first thirty days

Day 1 – Day 30 Concrete. In order. Start the external clocks first. Starting Monday

Days 1–2 — things that cost hours and buy weeks

  • Fix the pricing-page tense so the site claims no families you do not have
  • Name yourself as the accountable person in INCIDENT_RUNBOOK.md, commit it
  • Begin trademark clearance on the name
  • Send the §5 brief to two or three data-protection lawyers; ask each the deceased-persons question
  • Email three candidate security reviewers for availability and cost in week 6
  • Ask two disability organisations about a paid accessibility review in week 7

Days 3–7 — decide what is yours to decide

  • Work through §5 decisions 2, 5, 6 and 7; write each into the company decision log with its reasoning
  • Draft the prohibitions as customer-facing terms (§17, instrument 1)
  • Choose the hosting provider and region
  • List every development secret that must be replaced

Days 8–14 — the biggest engineering piece

  • Production authentication: OIDC or passkeys, MFA enrolment, and password reset
  • Real secrets in a secret manager; no dev-only-… string survives
  • Deploy to a real environment for the first time, with synthetic data only

Days 15–21 — make the unproven things proven

  • Run all eight integrations, in the §6 order, checking the stated first thing for each
  • Record your real unit costs as you go — this is the week the §2 worksheet gets filled in
  • Wire Stripe webhook signature verification; test a payment and a refund end to end
  • Compare the local extractive composer against the hosted one, and decide whether you need the hosted one at all

Days 22–30 — the things that are skipped

  • Encryption at rest with a customer-managed key
  • A real malware scanner in the upload path
  • Backups configured, and a restore performed into a clean environment and timed
  • A deletion performed end to end and verified against the database, not the receipt
  • Monitoring and alerting that reach your phone
  • Inspect logs, error reports and the support view for memory content; fix anything found
  • Write the death runbook (§18) and read it aloud once
  • Set the provisional price and update the pricing page
At the end of day 30 you should have

A deployed system with real authentication, proven integrations, known unit costs, a price, working billing, and every external clock running. What you will not have is a single real memory in the database — and that is correct. Weeks 5–8 close the launch gate; the first family comes after it, not before.
