3  The five structural problems

Not risks — risks are in §18. These are properties of the category that will not go away no matter how well you execute. Each one has an answer. None of the answers is “work harder”.

Problem 1 — The refusal does not demo

A voice clone sells itself in fifteen seconds. Your most important behaviour is declining to answer, which is invisible in a screenshot and reads as a limitation to anyone who has not thought about it for ten minutes. You are selling the absence of a failure, and absences do not demo. INFERENCE

What to do about it

  • Demo the pair, never the answer alone. Ask a question the archive can answer, then ask one it cannot, in the same breath. The second screen is the product. Your existing Figures 8 and 9 already do this — make it the fixed shape of every demo you ever give.
  • Demo to the storyteller, not the buyer. The parent is the one for whom the refusal is worth money. Watch an older person realise that the system will not put words in their mouth; that reaction is your marketing asset, and you cannot script it.
  • Show the click-through. Answer → cited sentence → transcript → the actual recording, in one continuous motion. That chain is a foreign-key walk, not a citation string, and it is the thing competitors cannot fake.

Problem 2 — The buyer is not the user

An adult child finds you, is moved, and pays. Then the entire value of the purchase depends on a seventy-eight-year-old who has never seen your website agreeing to spend hours recording, and then reviewing what was recorded. Your product correctly refuses to proceed without them — which is right, and which means your conversion rate is decided by a person you cannot market to. INFERENCE

What to do about it

  • Build the asking artifact. The hardest moment in this product is a child asking a parent. Give them the words: a letter, a card, a page written to be forwarded, that explains what this is and — crucially — what it refuses to do. This is a product feature, not a marketing asset, and it does not exist yet.
  • Make the first session absurdly easy. No account for the parent on day one if you can avoid it. A phone call, a scheduled session, someone else driving.
  • Measure the funnel that matters: payment → storyteller consents → first recording → first approved memory. That last step is your real activation event. Everything before it is a promise.

Problem 3 — There is no occasion

Everyone intends to do this and almost nobody does, which is the observation the product is founded on. The uncomfortable corollary: the same force that creates the market suppresses the purchase. Intent without a trigger produces a bookmarked website and no revenue. INFERENCE

What to do about it

Stop trying to manufacture urgency — you cannot, and attempting it lands you squarely in the guilt mechanics the product refuses. Instead, attach to occasions other people already own:

  • A milestone birthday — seventieth, eightieth
  • Retirement
  • A diagnosis, or a hospice admission
  • Moving out of a long-held home
  • A first grandchild
  • A significant anniversary
  • Estate or will preparation
  • Citizenship, or a return visit to a country left behind

Every one of those moments is already attended by somebody — a wealth adviser, a hospice social worker, a senior-living director, a family lawyer. That is the argument for §10, and it is the single most important strategic idea in this document after the pricing conclusion in §2.

Problem 4 — You have deliberately removed your retention levers

The standard subscription playbook retains customers by bringing them back: digests, reminders, streaks, nudges. Your product has no notification scheduler at all, on purpose, because reminders about a parent's mortality become guilt with almost no effort. VERIFIED

That is the correct decision and I would not reverse it. But be clear about what it costs: an archive that is finished is an archive nobody opens, and a subscription nobody opens is a subscription somebody eventually cancels.

What to do about it

  • Sell custody, not engagement. Nobody expects to visit their safe-deposit box monthly. Price and describe this as preservation. Then do not measure daily actives — you will panic at a number that means nothing here.
  • Let the family be the retention. Each additional family member with access is another person for whom cancelling is unthinkable. Members-per-archive is a retention metric; sessions-per-week is not.
  • Growth, not nudges. An archive can gain new chapters years later. Offer that when the family initiates, and only then — a returning customer is welcome, a chased one is a violation of the thing you built.
  • Handle the true test honestly: the renewal that arrives after the storyteller has died. Decide the policy now, in §17, while it is abstract. You will not think clearly about it the first time it happens to a real family.

Problem 5 — The verdict arrives years late

A family cannot tell whether the archive is any good until they need it — which may be a decade away, and often after the person has died. This breaks two loops at once: word of mouth is slow, because nobody has the experience that makes them evangelise; and your quality feedback is slow, because the failure mode — a thin archive, the wrong questions asked, the important story never told — only becomes visible when it is far too late to fix. INFERENCE

What to do about it

  • Manufacture an early verdict. Deliver something the family holds within weeks: the printed biography draft, a short edited audio piece, a set of stories read at a birthday. An artefact that lands while everyone is alive is what converts a customer into an advocate a decade early.
  • Grade the archive, not the software. Build a coverage view — which decades, which relationships, which themes are thin. That turns an unmeasurable outcome into a visible gap the family can close now.
  • Interview quality is the actual product. Two archives with identical software differ enormously based on what was asked. Treat the question set as your core IP, version it, and improve it deliberately. This is also what the concierge phase in §2 is for.
Read these five together

They all point the same direction: do not run this as a self-serve consumer subscription. Sell high-value archives through people who own the occasion, deliver something tangible early, and let custody — not engagement — carry retention. Parts II and III are that plan.
