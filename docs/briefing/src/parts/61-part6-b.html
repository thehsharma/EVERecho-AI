A closing note

You asked me to guide you to a unicorn. I have given you the arithmetic that outcome requires, the sequence that makes it possible, and the places where I think it most likely breaks. Here is what I actually believe.

The hardest thing about this company is not the technology, and you have already proved that by finishing the part everyone else fakes. It is that you have chosen a business whose central promise is a refusal — and refusals do not demo, do not go viral, and do not respond to the ordinary levers. Every structural problem in §3 traces back to that single choice.

It is still the right choice, and not for sentimental reasons. It is the only version of this product that institutions can adopt, that reluctant storytellers will fill honestly, and that survives the moment when the first simulation company is publicly disgraced. You are not building a safer version of a category. You are building the version that is left standing.

What I would hold you to, if I were actually sitting beside you:

  • Do not choose a cheap price. It is the one decision that forecloses everything you asked me about, and it is usually made in an afternoon by someone who feels awkward asking for money.
  • Do not put a real memory in that database before the gate in §7 closes. Not one. Not your own grandmother's.
  • Bind the refusal while it is easy — before investors, before an acquirer, before a bad quarter makes it feel negotiable.
  • Keep doing the interviews yourself long past the point it feels like a good use of your time. It is where the product actually comes from.
  • Publish what is not finished. The readiness document, the gaps, the things that never ran. It is your strangest asset and your most durable one.

And one honest thing about the word in your question. Nobody can hand you a unicorn, and this document has not tried to. What it has tried to do is make sure that if this company does not become one, it will be for reasons outside your control — not because you priced it cheaply in month two, launched before it was safe, or traded away the promise in a difficult week.

A thousand families with an intact promise is a real company and a good life's work. Fifty thousand is the arithmetic in §2. Both begin the same way: fix a sentence on your pricing page, name yourself accountable, send the brief to counsel, and start the clock.

Every claim in this document is labelled with its evidence class. Nothing here is a market figure I could not verify, a statistic I could not source, or a projection presented as a fact. It was written to the same standard as the product it describes, which seemed like the only defensible way to write it.
