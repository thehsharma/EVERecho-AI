4  The three assets you actually have

Section 3 was the honest accounting of what is hard. This is the honest accounting of what is rare — and all three of these are more valuable than the traction you do not yet have.

Asset 1 — The refusal is a position, and the market is moving toward it

You are not a slightly-safer version of the simulation products. You are the opposite category, and you can say so in one sentence that a stranger understands: we record what a living person actually said, we show you where every sentence came from, and we will never pretend to be them.

This is worth more than it looks, for three reasons:

  • Every bad story about a griefbot is your advertisement. You do not need to attack anyone. You need to be the obvious thing a journalist calls for the contrasting quote, and the obvious thing a reader searches for afterwards.
  • It is the only version institutions can touch. A hospice, a university archive, a bank's private-client arm, a religious community — none of them can put their name on a product that simulates the dead. The refusal is not a constraint on your enterprise sales; it is the precondition for having any. INFERENCE
  • It is enforced, not promised. Anyone can claim restraint. You can point at a policy compiler that refuses the document, a function that discards a first-person answer, and a test that fails if someone removes either. That is a difference you can demonstrate in a meeting.
The cost of this position

You will spend the rest of the company's life being mistaken for the thing you refuse to be. Budget for it: a permanent, prominent, plainly-worded explanation, repeated more often than feels necessary, forever. The Trust and consent page already does this well — treat it as a primary surface, not a footer link.

Asset 2 — The machinery underneath might be the larger company

Strip away the family archive and look at what you built: a consent document that compiles to an enforceable policy; a pure authorisation function whose obligations become part of the SQL query rather than a filter applied afterwards; retrieval that cannot see what the viewer may not know exists; a verification step that drops any sentence its citation does not support; forced tenant isolation at the database; and deletion that is resumable and produces a receipt.

That is a general answer to a question a great many organisations are being asked right now: how do you let an AI system read sensitive material without it leaking, inventing, or exceeding what the subject agreed to? Adjacent buyers exist in oral history and archives, hospice and palliative care, estate and private-client services, journalism and documentary research, and any regulated setting where an answer must cite and abstain. Decision — do not make this one yet

The temptation, in about month four, will be to pivot from families to infrastructure because enterprise revenue looks closer. Resist it until you have run §8. Two reasons: the consumer product is where you learn what the consent model must actually do, and an infrastructure company with no application is a crowded, undifferentiated place to arrive.

Recommendation: treat this as an option you hold, not a plan you execute. Keep the packages cleanly separated (they already are), take an enterprise conversation if one walks in, and revisit deliberately at the §16 branch point — not in a discouraged week.

Asset 3 — Verifiable honesty, in a market with almost none of it

You have a document that says “Not ready for a real family's memories”, a table listing eight integrations that were written and never executed, and a product that answers a question it cannot support with a fixed sentence declining to answer. Nobody does this. It is worth a great deal, in three specific ways.

With Why it converts
Storytellers They are deciding whether to hand you the most private material they have. Restraint they can verify is the only argument that works on someone genuinely reluctant — and the reluctant ones are the ones with the stories worth keeping.
Institutions Procurement, ethics committees and compliance teams are trying to find out what you are hiding. Handing over the readiness document unprompted collapses months of that.
Investors You have no traction. What you have instead is evidence about the founder: a complete, tested, documented product with the hard parts finished and the gaps named. For a pre-traction round, that is the diligence. INFERENCE
A move I would make in month one

Publish the production-readiness document. Publicly, on the site, including the verdict and the list of things that were never run. Almost nobody will do this, which is exactly why it works: it makes every other claim you make credible, and it costs you nothing you were going to get away with anyway. If it later becomes commercially awkward, that is a signal you have started overstating — which is the failure mode this company most needs an alarm for.
