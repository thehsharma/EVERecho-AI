2  What a unicorn actually requires

You asked for a path to a billion-dollar company. The useful response is not encouragement; it is arithmetic. The arithmetic has one clear conclusion, and it should change what you build next.

Said once, plainly

Nobody can promise you a unicorn, and any adviser who does is selling something. Outcomes at that scale are rare enough that no plan produces one on demand. What a plan can do is make the outcome possible instead of arithmetically impossible — and most companies foreclose it early, by choosing a price. That is the whole content of this section.

Working backwards from the number

A $1 billion valuation is a multiple applied to revenue. Software businesses have traded across a wide band depending on growth, margin and the market's mood, and I am not going to invent a current figure. Take 10× forward revenue as a working assumption and treat the answer as an order of magnitude, not a target. ASSUMPTION

Target valuation$1,000,000,000
Working revenue multiple÷ 10
Annual recurring revenue required$100,000,000

So: roughly $100M of annual recurring revenue. Everything now turns on one variable — what one archive pays you per year.

The conclusion, which is the whole point of the section

A low-priced consumer subscription cannot reach this outcome for this product. 833,000 families paying every year, in a category where people demonstrably postpone the thing for decades, is not a plan — it is a wish. Price high, sell through institutions, or both. Everything in Part III follows from this line. INFERENCE

The margin trap inside model C

Model C reaches high revenue per archive by putting a person in the loop: someone conducts the interview well, someone helps an eighty-year-old through the review queue. That works, and it is probably how you should start. But a business whose costs scale with headcount earns a services multiple, not a software multiple — and at a services multiple, $100M of revenue is not a billion-dollar company.

concierge-led product-led ───────────── ─────────── high revenue per archive high revenue per archive costs scale with people ──▶ costs scale with software gross margin ~40–60% gross margin ~75–85% services multiple software multiple │ ▲ └──── use it to LEARN, then productise ──┘ the repeatable parts

The resolution is sequencing, not choosing. Run concierge deliberately and temporarily: it is the fastest way to learn what a good interview actually is, and every hour a human spends is a specification for something to automate. Then move each repeatable piece into the product and watch gross margin climb. If gross margin is not rising by the fiftieth archive, the concierge model has become the company. INFERENCE

The line that can quietly bankrupt this company

Storage is perpetual; subscriptions are not. You are promising to preserve something. If a family pays for three years and stops, and you keep the archive because deleting a dead parent's recordings feels monstrous, you have taken on an unfunded liability that grows with every customer you win. Video makes it far worse than audio — the same hour of material can be one to two orders of magnitude larger.

Two structural answers, and you should take both: (1) default to audio, price video as a deliberate upgrade; (2) fund preservation explicitly — either the subscription must continue for the archive to be hosted (and export is always free, so nothing is held hostage), or a one-time preservation payment is priced to cover storage for a stated term. Whichever you choose, say it in plain words on the pricing page before anyone pays. INFERENCE

A perpetual-care endowment is the model cemeteries use, and it may carry trust, escrow or reserve obligations depending on jurisdiction. Put it on the list for counsel in §5 rather than designing it yourself. UNKNOWN — legal question

What I would actually aim at

Not $100M. Aim at the milestone that makes the next one possible, and let the arithmetic above tell you only one thing: do not choose a cheap price. A cheap price is the single decision that forecloses the outcome you asked me about, and it is usually made casually, in an afternoon, by a founder who feels awkward asking for money.
