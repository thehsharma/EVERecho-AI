Money

Whether to raise it, how to raise it if you do, and the staged path the arithmetic in §2 actually implies.

14  Bootstrap or raise

The generic answer is “raise, because you want a large outcome.” For this business the generic answer is wrong about the timing and right about the destination.

The case for not raising yet

  • Nothing is racing you. There is no network effect, no land grab, no winner-take-all dynamic. Nobody wins this market by being six months earlier; they win it by being trusted, and trust does not respond to capital.
  • The concierge phase earns money from the first customer. Model C pricing on ten families is real revenue during the period you would otherwise be fundraising.
  • Capital creates pressure toward exactly the features you have prohibited. A board watching flat engagement will, sincerely and reasonably, ask why you will not add the voice. Every prohibition in this product is a growth constraint, which is the whole point, and money makes constraints feel like problems.
  • You have no answer yet to the questions money would make urgent. Raising before §8 means spending someone else's money to discover your price and your channel — which you can discover for far less.

The case for raising

  • You asked for a billion-dollar outcome. Reaching $100M of recurring revenue without external capital is rare enough that planning for it would be planning on an exception. INFERENCE
  • The launch gate costs real money. An independent security review, counsel in multiple jurisdictions, encryption infrastructure, a real scanner and hosting are not small sums, and none of them is optional.
  • Channel sales cycles are long. Estate advisers and senior-living operators move over quarters. That requires runway, not hustle.
  • Preservation is a balance-sheet promise. Committing to hold something for decades is more credible from a company with capital than from one person with a card.
Decision — the sequence I recommend

Bootstrap through §8. Then raise a small round on the strength of ten paying families.

Ten families who paid full price, with a measured cost to serve, a real activation rate and one channel that produced introductions, is a stronger fundraising asset than any deck you could write instead — and it costs you three months rather than three months of meetings. It also inverts the power in the conversation, which matters enormously for the next point.

And the part almost nobody plans: choose investors by whether they will accept the §17 governance terms in writing. An investor who treats the prohibitions as a phase is not a cheaper investor — they are a future fight over the thing your company exists to be. Raise less, from people who understand what they are funding.

15  If you raise

The narrative

Do not lead with the family archive. Lead with the thesis, because the thesis is what makes the archive interesting:

“The industry is racing to simulate dead people. That is going to fail publicly and badly, and when it does, every serious institution — every hospice, university, bank and family — will need the version that refuses. We built that version first, and the hard part is not the product. It is the consent and provenance machinery underneath it, which took eight months and which nobody copies in a quarter.”

The order to present in

  1. The problem, told through the failure mode: a grieving family believing a fabricated answer.
  2. The refusal, as a position rather than a policy — and the demo pair from §3, live.
  3. What is actually built: 279 tests, forced isolation, 100% claim-to-citation, zero permission leaks, all measured.
  4. The ten families: price paid, activation rate, cost to serve, channel origin.
  5. Why the machinery is the moat, and the optionality in §4.
  6. The channel thesis, with the one partner that worked.
  7. The arithmetic from §2, honestly — including why you are not building a cheap consumer subscription.
  8. The readiness document, handed over unprompted.
  9. What the money buys, tied to the §16 stage you are trying to reach.

What you will be asked, and what is actually true

The questionThe honest answer
“Why not add voice cloning? Users will want it.”The one that matters. Answer: because the archive is only worth filling if the storyteller believes it will never be used that way, and a company that would add it later is one they must reason about today. Then point out that it is refused by a policy compiler and a test, not by a promise — which is why institutions can buy it. Prepare this answer word for word. You will give it in every meeting.
“Isn't the market small?”Show §2. The answer is not that the market is large; it is that revenue per archive can be large, and here is the price ten families actually paid.
“What's your CAC?”UNKNOWN, and say so. Then say precisely how you will find out and by when. A founder who says “I don't know yet, here's the experiment” is more credible than one with a confident invented number — and this is a company whose entire pitch is not inventing numbers.
“Why won't they churn once the archive is finished?”Custody, not engagement (§3, problem 4). Members per archive is the retention driver. Be honest that twelve-month retention is not yet measured.
“Why can't a large platform do this tomorrow?”They can build the features. What they will not do is adopt the refusal, because it is directly opposed to their engagement and data incentives — and without the refusal, the product is the thing you are not.
“What happens when someone dies?”Answer operationally, not philosophically: the succession directive, the runbook, and the honest note that no automatic transition can ever be triggered by inactivity.
