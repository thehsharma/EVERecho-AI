Part I

The pitch

What this is, who it is for, and the single decision that everything else in the product follows from.

1  The problem nobody solves in time

Almost every family intends to record their parents' and grandparents' stories. Almost none of them do it before it is too late.

The intention is universal and the follow-through is rare, for reasons that are practical rather than emotional. Sitting down with a camera feels like an occasion, and occasions get postponed. When somebody does record, the result is four hours of unedited video on a hard drive that nobody watches, in which nothing is findable. And the interviewer never knows what to ask, so the same five questions produce the same five anecdotes, while the things the family will actually want — why a decision was made, what a house smelled like, who was in the room — go unasked.

Then the person dies, and what remains is a shoebox of photographs nobody can label and a set of half-remembered stories that begin to drift. Within a generation, the drift is total. The grandchildren inherit a name and a face and almost nothing else.

The technology industry's answer to this has been, with grim consistency, to propose simulating the dead person. Voice clones. Video avatars. Chatbots that speak in the first person as somebody's late mother. This is a product built from the family's grief rather than from the person's life, and it makes a promise it cannot keep: that the thing you are talking to knows anything. It does not. It is producing plausible sentences, and the sentences it invents are indistinguishable, to the person who is grieving, from the ones that are real.

The failure mode that matters

A grieving family asks a simulated parent a question the archive has no answer to, and receives a fluent, confident, entirely fabricated response. They believe it. They repeat it. It becomes family history. The system has not merely failed — it has corrupted the record it was built to preserve.

EverEcho starts from the opposite end. Record the person while they are alive, with their active participation and their explicit permission, at the granularity of individual stories. Structure what they said so it can be found. And when anyone asks the archive a question, answer only from what was actually recorded, show the source for every sentence, and say plainly when there is nothing to say.

2  What EverEcho is

A private family archive built from a living person's own recordings, in which every AI-assisted answer is traceable to a specific thing that person actually said.

Four things happen, in order.

The person tells their stories

Guided interview sessions ask questions that go somewhere — not "tell me about your childhood" but the follow-up that gets to the detail. Recordings are uploaded, scanned, transcribed, and turned into a set of individual memories: a title, a passage, a date range where one is known, the people and places mentioned, and a link back to the exact span of transcript it came from.

The person reviews everything

Nothing is visible to the family until the storyteller has seen it and approved it. A memory can be edited, its date corrected, its participants adjusted, or it can be rejected outright and never appear. The review queue is the storyteller's own screen, and only theirs.

The family reads, browses and asks

Approved memories become a timeline, a set of people and places, and a short biography assembled from what was said. Anyone with permission can also ask the archive a question in ordinary language — "What did she say about leaving Delhi?" — and get an answer composed only from cited passages, each one clickable back to the transcript and, from there, to the recording.

The person keeps control of all of it, permanently

Permission is not granted once at signup. It is a living document: which categories of material may be shared, with whom, for what purpose, until when. It can be narrowed or revoked at any time, and revocation takes effect everywhere — including on work already queued in the background, which re-checks consent before it writes anything. Deletion is real deletion, executed in resumable steps, and it produces a receipt.
